\subsection{Dataset}

The KneeOA dataset consists of 1{,}055 grayscale knee X-ray images distributed across
three splits: 750 training, 159 validation, and 146 test images.
Each image is a $224 \times 224$ pixel PNG in single-channel (grayscale) format.
The binary label indicates the presence (1) or absence (0) of knee osteoarthritis (OA).

The class distribution is consistent across splits: approximately 67\% healthy (class~0)
and 33\% OA (class~1), yielding a minority-class ratio of roughly 1:2.
Filenames encode a patient identifier and laterality (e.g.\ \texttt{9069761R.png}),
indicating left or right knee.
Of the 1{,}055 images, 334 patient IDs appear twice, corresponding to both knees of the
same patient being included.
A cross-split leakage check confirmed that no patient ID appears in more than one split,
so the train/val/test partitions are patient-disjoint.

Pixel intensity statistics reveal variation in brightness across the dataset.
The global training-set mean is approximately 155 out of 255, with a standard deviation
of roughly 50 (in 8-bit range).
Per-image brightness (mean pixel value) varies substantially between images
($\sigma \approx 26$ across training images), as does contrast (per-image pixel standard
deviation, $\sigma \approx 15$).

\subsection{Classification Challenges}

Several intrinsic characteristics of the dataset complicate the classification task.

\textbf{Class imbalance.}
The 2:1 healthy-to-OA ratio introduces a risk of the model optimising for the majority
class. A standard cross-entropy loss would permit high accuracy while systematically
missing OA cases. Because false negatives carry clinical cost (missed diagnosis),
we address this by using a weighted loss function and monitoring F1-score and recall on
class~1 rather than overall accuracy.

\textbf{Image variability.}
Visual inspection reveals considerable variation in exposure (brightness) and contrast
across images. Some images appear notably darker or lighter than the dataset average.
This variation is not diagnostic and may confound the classifier, motivating contrast
and brightness augmentation.

\textbf{Laterality.}
The dataset contains both left and right knee images. Left and right knees are mirror
images of each other; the OA label is unaffected by side. Horizontal flipping is
therefore clinically valid as an augmentation, unlike domains (e.g.\ chest X-rays) where
laterality carries diagnostic information.

\textbf{Foreign objects.}
A manual inspection identified 15 images containing surgical hardware (screws, plates)
or film markers. These objects are distributed across both classes and all splits.
Their influence on the classifier cannot be determined at the preprocessing stage;
they are retained in the dataset and flagged for post-hoc analysis of misclassified
cases.

\subsection{Image Handling}

\textbf{Downsampling.}
All images are resized from $224 \times 224$ to $128 \times 128$ pixels using Bilinear
resampling with antialiasing. This is recommended for downscaling high-quality
images~\cite{Torchvision}.
The resolution reduction lowers memory and compute requirements for subsequent tasks
at an acceptable loss of fine-grained detail.

\textbf{Normalization.}
Images are normalized using the global mean and standard deviation computed over all
pixels in the training set (mean $\approx 0.607$, std $\approx 0.194$ on the $[0,1]$
scale after \texttt{ToTensor}).
This choice is preferred over fixed values (e.g.\ $\mu=0.5$, $\sigma=0.5$) because it
reflects the actual data distribution, and over per-sample normalization because that
would destroy genuine brightness differences between images that may carry diagnostic
signal.
Validation and test images are normalized with the \emph{training} statistics to avoid
any form of data leakage.

\textbf{Augmentation.}
Data augmentation is applied \emph{online} during training: each batch is augmented
on-the-fly by the \texttt{DataLoader}, producing a new random variant of each image
every epoch. This provides greater variety than a fixed set of pre-generated augmented
copies, avoiding overfitting.
3 augmentation operations are applied sequentially before resizing. These variate the
geometry of the XRAY image, and simulate variation in patient positioning.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random horizontal flip} (p\,=\,0.5): clinically valid because left and
        right knees are mirror images; doubles the effective dataset size.
  \item \textbf{Random affine rotation} ($\pm 5^\circ$): simulates small patient
        positioning differences; kept small to avoid unrealistic joint orientations.
  \item \textbf{Random affine translation} ($\pm 3^\circ$): also simulates small patient
        positioning differences; kept small to avoid unrealistic joint orientations.
\end{itemize}
2 augmentation operations are applied sequentially after resizing. These variate the
pixel intensities and are best applied after an interpolation step (subsampling).
Interpolation after pixel augmentations might affect realism of the image.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Color jitter} (brightness $\pm 10\%$, contrast $\pm 15\%$): reflects
        the observed inter-image exposure variability and encourages the model to learn
        structure rather than absolute intensity.
\end{itemize}
Augmentation is applied exclusively to the training set.
Validation and test sets are left unmodified so that evaluation metrics provide an
unbiased estimate of generalisation performance under real-world conditions.
